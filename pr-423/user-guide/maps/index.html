Maps and Basemaps¶

A project holds one or more maps (Home › New Map), each with its own layers, basemap, projection, bookmarks and view settings. Maps open as tabs above the map view.

Navigating¶

Drag to pan and scroll to zoom; Home (or Full Extent) shows all the data. Map › Navigate has fixed zoom steps, Previous and Next extents, Bookmarks (the World Atlas has World, Europe, Africa, Americas and East Asia) and Go To XY, which pans to a longitude and latitude. New Bookmark… saves the current view under a name, with an optional description of the place. The Bookmarks menu removes a bookmark with the button beside it, and exports or imports them as a .rgisbookmarks file. Imported bookmarks show the same place on a map in another projection; places that projection can't show are skipped. Type a scale in the status bar to jump to it. More navigation tools are under View effects below.

Manage Bookmarks (at the foot of the Bookmarks menu, or View › Windows › Bookmarks) opens the Bookmarks pane: every map's bookmarks, grouped by map (the map in view in the accent colour), with a box that filters them by name or description. On each bookmark, Zoom To shows its place at its scale and Pan To centres it at the current scale; a bookmark of another map takes the map in view to the same place. Update saves the current view into it (only on its own map, in view), and Delete removes it. The chevron shows its description: click it to write or edit it. Double-click a name to rename it, and right-click it to rename, describe, update, move it up or down, or delete it. Everything the pane does is a bookmark.* command and undoes with Ctrl+Z.

Find Place (map.findPlace) looks up a place or an address by name with OpenStreetMap's Nominatim (or a Photon server, or a geocoder of your own given by its address) and zooms to the one picked. The search goes to that server over the network; see Geocoding addresses for whole tables.

The search box at the foot of the left pane searches the map as you type: a latitude and longitude (40.7, -74.0, 40.7N 74W or 40°42'46"N 74°0'21"W; latitude first unless the letters or a number past ±90 say otherwise), the map's layers and bookmarks by name, and the values in its layers' attribute tables (the best few per layer, by the feature's name where it has one). Places come from Nominatim when you press Enter on Search places, since the public servers don't allow searching as you type. The arrow keys move through the list and Enter picks: a layer is zoomed to, a feature selected and zoomed to, and a coordinate or a place is marked with a pin until the box is cleared (✕ or Esc). map.search runs the same search from the command line or a script, and goes to a hit with "go":

rustgis-cli run --sample --cmd 'map.search={"query":"Tokyo","go":true}'

Right-click the map for a menu about that place. Its first row shows the latitude and longitude; click it to copy them. Below it, Copy As GeoJSON copies the place as a point feature, Center Map Here and Zoom In Here move the view, and Directions To Here and Directions From Here open the Directions pane. Quick Analysis runs a tool at the place: a buffer of 500 m, 1 km or 5 km (¼, 1 or 5 miles when the Directions pane is set to miles; gp.bufferPoint), a viewshed of 2, 5 or 15 km or a line of sight on an elevation raster in the map, and Open In Geoprocessing… opens the Buffer tool. View In Google Maps and View In Google Earth open the place in your browser at about the same zoom.

Directions¶

Directions (Home › Navigate) plans a route between places and follows it turn by turn, with the public Valhalla server run by FOSSGIS on OpenStreetMap data. Pick Drive, Bike or Walk, then set the start and the destination: type an address (Enter looks it up with Nominatim) or a latitude and longitude, press the ⌖ button and click the map, or right-click the map and choose Directions From Here or Directions To Here. + Add Stop adds places on the way (up to 20 in all), Swap reverses the trip, and a drive can avoid tolls, highways or ferries; Miles gives distances in miles and feet.

Routes are drawn as soon as every place is set. Between two places the server offers up to two other ways, drawn grey: pick one in the list or click it on the map. The steps list each maneuver with its arrow, instruction and distance; click one to zoom to it. Simulate replays the route as a drive the map follows, with the next maneuver and its distance in a banner across the top of the map, at 1× to 16× speed (pause, drag the slider to jump along, End to stop). Save as Layer adds the route as a line layer, and its maneuvers as a point layer, to style, export (GPX, GeoJSON…) or keep with the project.

The first time, the pane asks before anything is sent: planning a route sends its places to the routing server, and looking a place up sends its name to Nominatim. Travel times don't include live traffic, and the public server is shared, so it answers light use only.

map.route plans a route from the command line, a script or the AI Assistant, with places given as [lon, lat], "lat, lon" or a name to look up, and adds it and its steps as layers; ui.pane.directions with from, to (and via, mode) opens the pane on a route. map.route sends its places to the same public servers (names to Nominatim, at most five a call, a second apart; coordinates to FOSSGIS's Valhalla), so the AI Assistant asks before running it:

rustgis-cli run --sample --cmd 'map.route={"from":"Brandenburg Gate, Berlin","to":"Alexanderplatz, Berlin","mode":"bike"}'

Basemaps¶

Home › Add › Basemap offers five built-in basemaps drawn from a world outline (Paper, Midnight, Ocean, Atlas, Blank), which need no network, and web tile layers over them:

  • Raster tiles from an XYZ or TMS template (https://…/{z}/{x}/{y}.png; {-y} for rows from the south, {s} for subdomains, {q} for Bing-style quadkeys) or a WMS through {bbox-epsg-3857}. PNG, JPEG and WebP.
  • Vector tiles (Mapbox Vector Tiles, .pbf) from a template or a TileJSON address, drawn in a built-in style that knows the OpenMapTiles, Protomaps and Shortbread schemas.
  • PMTiles archives (.pmtiles) of raster or vector tiles: a web address, read a tile at a time by byte ranges (the server must allow range requests, and CORS for the web app), or on the desktop a file (Tile Archive File…, Add Data, or drop it on the window). A vector archive that is a basemap is drawn in the built-in style; any other is drawn with each of its layers in a colour of its own.
  • MBTiles files (.mbtiles) of raster or vector tiles, drawn as PMTiles archives are: on the desktop read in place a tile at a time (Tile Archive File…, Add Data, or drop it on the window); in the web app added with Add Data and held in memory for the session (a project keeps the layer, and asks for the file again).
  • Map styles: a MapLibre style document (style.json), with its vector and raster sources. RustGIS draws background, fill, line, circle and raster layers, point labels with their icons from the style's sprite (SDF icons in their icon-color), and fill patterns; it evaluates the style's expressions and zoom functions. Line labels and shields along roads, line patterns, hillshade and 3D extrusion are not drawn (extrusions draw flat). A label shows the lines of its text that the map's font can draw: a style's "Syria / سوريا" shows as "Syria".

The gallery's Web Basemaps need no API key: the OpenFreeMap vector maps (Liberty, Bright, Positron, Dark, Fiord) and USGS Imagery. Choosing one replaces the other web basemaps. For many more (OpenStreetMap, Esri, NASA and others), follow Install the Basemaps plugin under them, which opens the Plugin Manager at the Basemaps plugin; it adds a tab of public basemaps. Add any other with Add Tile Layer… at the bottom of the Basemap gallery. The gallery lists the map's tile layers: show or hide them, change their opacity and order, or remove them. The Layers pane lists them too, under the map's layers and above the basemap: tick one to show or hide it, and right-click it for its opacity, order and Remove. Overlays (labels, roads) stay on top of the basemaps; choosing a built-in basemap removes the web basemaps but keeps the overlays. Tile layers are saved with the project, and their credits are shown in the corner of the map and drawn on exports.

Tiles load in the background and the map fills in as they arrive, with coarser tiles standing in meanwhile; exports and rustgis-cli wait for them. On the globe and in other projections the tiles are reprojected. From scripts: map.addTiles, map.removeTiles, map.setTiles and map.tiles.

Worldview¶

The world outline and the World Atlas sample's countries come in two worldviews: Natural Earth's default borders, and China's (Natural Earth's China point of view: Taiwan, Aksai Chin and South Tibet are part of China). RustGIS picks China's when the system's time zone is mainland China's (such as Asia/Shanghai) or its first locale is zh-CN; nothing is looked up over the network. Choose one in Settings › Language › Worldview, set RUSTGIS_WORLDVIEW (default or china), or pass --worldview to rustgis-cli. An untouched sample project switches its countries at once; others keep the layer they have.

Projections¶

The flat map is drawn in Web Mercator by default. View › Projection switches it to another projection, and clicking the coordinate system in the status bar opens the Coordinate System dialog, which also takes a projected EPSG code (such as 3035, 32633 for UTM zone 33N or 2056 for Switzerland). A code whose projection method or datum grid RustGIS can't use (Polyconic such as 5880, Cassini, oblique Mercator, or a NAD27 code that needs a grid) is refused with the reason, rather than drawn in another projection:

Preset Kind
Equirectangular, Miller World, cylindrical
Mollweide, Lambert Cylindrical Equal Area, Equal Earth, Eckert IV World, equal-area
Robinson, Natural Earth, Winkel Tripel World, compromise
North / South Polar Stereographic Polar
Europe Equal Area (ETRS89 LAEA), USA Contiguous Albers Equal Area Regional, equal-area

Layers are projected on the fly. A feature that crosses the edge of a world projection (the meridian opposite its central one, such as 90°E in Equal Earth Americas) is cut there and drawn on both sides of the map. Each projection has an area of use: polar projections show their hemisphere poleward of 30°, regional ones their region, transverse Mercator (UTM) zones 30° either side of their meridian, and data outside it isn't drawn. Switching keeps the centre and scale, and the choice is saved with the map (map.projection from the command line). A raster already in the map's coordinate system draws without being reprojected. To open every new session in a projection, set it in Settings › Start-up › Projection. Robinson, Equal Earth, Natural Earth, Winkel Tripel and Eckert IV are computed by RustGIS itself on a sphere, so EPSG:8857 (Equal Earth) works too.

Globe¶

View › Globe shows the map as a 3D globe seen from space instead of the flat Web Mercator map, and back again. Drag to turn the globe, scroll to zoom, and use Full Extent to see the whole Earth. Every layer draws on the globe: vectors are clipped at the horizon and follow great circles, rasters are draped onto the sphere, and labels, selection, identify, measuring and editing work as on the flat map. The scale stays the same when you switch, and the choice is saved with each map in the project (map.projection from the command line).

While the globe turns or zooms, the map is drawn flat once and laid over the sphere by the graphics card, so it moves smoothly whatever the basemap; labels come back, and the map is drawn exactly, as soon as it stops.

The globe hangs in dark space, in the light theme too, with stars around it and now and then a shooting star passing behind it. View › Visual Effect › Stars turns them off. Exported maps and layout map frames print the globe in a light space instead, without stars, so a globe inset suits a light page (on the Midnight basemap the space stays dark).

View effects¶

The View tab adds effects drawn over the map: Illumination (day and night shading for a time, with twilight), Weather (haze, fog, rain, snow), Visual Effect (vignette, edges, the stars around the globe), Camera Effect (tilt-shift blur, film grain) and Postprocessing (brightness, contrast, saturation, sepia). The Color Vision simulator shows the map as people with protanopia, deuteranopia, tritanopia or achromatopsia see it (on screen only; exports are untouched).

Global shows a globe; Local a flat view centred where you are (azimuthal equidistant; not in the web app, which doesn't include that projection). Wireframe draws polygons as outlines, Depth Priority lifts a layer above others whatever its order, View Clipping hides features beyond a distance from the centre, and Speed Display draws without antialiasing. The Navigator adds a compass, zoom buttons and a pan pad; Camera shows and sets the position and scale; the Magnifier shows twice the map under the pointer. On the Home tab, First-Person navigation walks with W A S D and flies up and down with Q and E. The interface follows the system's light or dark setting; View › Display › Dark Mode switches it by hand.

View › Display › GPU Rendering (beta) draws the flat map on the GPU instead of the CPU: vector layers are tessellated once and re-projected per frame, so panning and zooming stay smooth on large datasets. It is off by default, and the setting is remembered across sessions (ui.map.gpu is the command; RUSTGIS_GPU=1 forces it on for development). The globe, view effects and the colour-vision simulator always render on the CPU, and exports are unaffected — the choice only changes the on-screen flat map.
