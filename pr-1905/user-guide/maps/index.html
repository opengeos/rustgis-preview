Maps and Basemaps¶

A project holds one or more maps (Home › Project › New Map), each with its own layers, basemap, projection, bookmarks and view settings. Maps open as tabs above the map view. In the Browser, right-click a map to rename, duplicate or delete it; the project always keeps at least one map. For selecting, grouping, reordering and copying layers between maps, see The window.

Navigating¶

Drag to pan and scroll to zoom; Home (or Full Extent) shows all the data. Home › Navigate has fixed zoom steps, Previous and Next extents, Bookmarks (the World Atlas has World, Europe, Africa, Americas and East Asia), and Locate: By Coordinates… pans to a longitude and latitude; By Place or Address… finds a place or address with a geocoder. New Bookmark… saves the current view under a name, with an optional description of the place. The Bookmarks menu removes a bookmark with the button beside it, and exports or imports them as a .rgisbookmarks file. Imported bookmarks show the same place on a map in another projection; places that projection can't show are skipped. Type a scale in the status bar to jump to it: the map zooms from the whole world in past 2:1, close enough for site plans at 1:20 and finds drawings at 1:1 (type 2:1 for twice life size). Beside it are the zoom level, bearing and pitch; click them to face north and look straight down again. More navigation tools are under View effects below.

Measurement units. In Home › Inspect › Measure, choose Metric or Imperial. The choice is remembered and updates existing distance and area measurements, the live map scale bar, elevation profiles, and distance labels and presets for label visibility and view clipping. Imperial readouts use feet or miles for length, and square feet, acres or square miles for area, according to size. Profile elevations and gain/loss use feet. Feet are international feet (0.3048 m). Scripts can set ui.measurementUnits={"units":"imperial"} or {"units":"metric"}; calling it without units toggles the choice. Distance presets still send metre values to commands. Editing tools, geoprocessing tools, directions and layout scale bars have their own explicit unit controls.

Zoom To Layer in a layer's right-click menu shows that layer's extent instead of the whole map. It works for feature and raster data, PMTiles archives, cloud-optimised GeoTIFFs, and service tile layers whose capabilities advertise geographic bounds, including WMS. WMS 1.3 geographic bounds and WMS 1.1 latitude/longitude bounds are inherited from parent layers when needed and saved with the project. Re-add a service layer saved by an older version to retain its bounds. Tile layers without known bounds (such as plain XYZ/TMS or MVT templates, styles and MBTiles archives) have no Zoom To Layer action unless bounds were supplied when adding them.

Rotating and tilting the map. Drag with the right mouse button, or hold Ctrl and drag with the Pan tool, to turn the map about the centre of the view (to the right turns it clockwise) and to tilt it (up tilts it further), as in MapLibre; or hold Shift and press Left or Right to turn it, Up or Down to tilt it. The north arrow turns with the map to point north and leans back as it tilts; click it to face north and look straight down again. The bearing (the compass direction the top of the view faces, in degrees clockwise from north) and the pitch (the tilt from looking straight down, 0 to 85 degrees) work as in MapLibre. They are saved with the project, its bookmarks and bookmark files, and exported maps are drawn turned and tilted. A tilted map keeps the scale at the centre of the view: nearer ground is larger, farther ground smaller and drawn coarser, fading into fog and, past about 72 degrees, a sky above the horizon. Labels stay upright, and those too far away to read are left out. Point markers stand upright, facing you, as MapLibre's circles do, smaller with distance (aggregated, masked, blended or diagrammed point layers, and layers of more than 50,000 points, keep their markers on the ground). Extruded layers (Style › Extrusion) stand up in perspective on a tilted map, as in MapLibre: walls lit by the way they face and darker toward their foot, roofs at their height, with the labels of extruded features on their roofs. While a tilted map moves, the picture drawn last is laid over the ground and the extrusions and markers are moved with it, and the map is drawn again once it settles. map.setBearing sets the bearing ({"bearing": 90} puts east at the top; {"by": 15} turns it further), map.setPitch the pitch ({"pitch": 60}, or {"by": 10}), map.resetNorth faces north and looks straight down, and map.goToXY takes a bearing and a pitch too. Page layout map frames take their own bearing and pitch (see Layouts), and the globe turns and tilts the same way: tilted, its camera swings back about the centre of view, so the horizon comes into sight past the curve of the Earth.

Terrain gives a tilted map relief, as MapLibre's terrain does: the map is draped over the ground's heights, labels and point markers stand on it, and nearer slopes hide what lies behind them. While the map moves, the relief is laid on a coarse grid, to keep up; once it settles, and in exported maps, on a grid four times finer each way, so a ridge's outline follows the ridge (the settled map fades in over the moving one in a quarter of a second). The heights come from a DEM raster layer of the project (heights in metres; the layer can stay hidden) or from elevation tiles: Terrarium (terrarium, as the free https://s3.amazonaws.com/elevation-tiles-prod/terrarium/{z}/{x}/{y}.png) or Mapbox Terrain-RGB (mapbox, also MapTiler's). View › Scene › Terrain turns it on from the active layer (make the DEM the active layer first) and off again; a notice names the layer and says to tilt the map. map.terrain sets it: {"layer": "DEM"}, or {} for the active raster layer when no terrain is on, or {"tiles": "https://…/{z}/{x}/{y}.png", "encoding": "terrarium"}, with "exaggeration" (0 to 20, default 1) to stretch the heights; {"exaggeration": 2} alone changes only that, and {"off": true} flattens the map again. Terrain is saved with the project and shows in exported maps; a flat (untilted) view, the globe and other projections stay flat, extruded layers are not lifted onto the relief yet, and identify and selection still pick on the flat ground.

MapLibre map links carry a view in their address, as #zoom/latitude/longitude/bearing/pitch (https://…/#15.5/40.7484/-73.9857/30/60). Go To Map Link (map.goToLink {"url": …}) takes such a link, or the part after its #, to that place, zoom, bearing and pitch, and map.link gives the current view the same way. The web app reads one from its own address (see Installation).

Manage Bookmarks (at the foot of the Bookmarks menu, or View › Windows › Bookmarks) opens the Bookmarks pane: every map's bookmarks, grouped by map (the map in view in the accent colour), with a box that filters them by name or description. On each bookmark, Zoom To shows its place at its scale and Pan To centres it at the current scale; a bookmark of another map takes the map in view to the same place. Update saves the current view into it (only on its own map, in view), and Delete removes it. The chevron shows its description: click it to write or edit it. Double-click a name to rename it, and right-click it to rename, describe, update, move it up or down, or delete it. Bookmark edits run bookmark.* commands and undo with Ctrl+Z; Zoom To and Pan To change the view without adding an undo step.

Home › Navigate › Locate › By Place or Address… (map.findPlace) looks up a place or an address by name with OpenStreetMap's Nominatim (or a Photon server, PDOK's Locatieserver for Dutch addresses and places, the U.S. Census Bureau for U.S. street addresses (desktop/CLI, or a CORS-enabled proxy on the web), or a geocoder of your own given by its address) and zooms to the one picked. The search goes to that server over the network; see Geocoding addresses for whole tables.

The search box at the foot of the left pane searches the map as you type: a latitude and longitude (40.7, -74.0, 40.7N 74W or 40°42'46"N 74°0'21"W; latitude first unless the letters or a number past ±90 say otherwise), a UTM position or an MGRS reference (see Coordinate readouts), the map's layers and bookmarks by name, and the values in its layers' attribute tables (the best few per layer, by the feature's name where it has one). Places come from Nominatim when you press Enter on Search places, since the public servers don't allow searching as you type. The arrow keys move through the list and Enter picks: a layer is zoomed to, a feature selected and zoomed to, and a coordinate or a place is marked with a pin until the box is cleared (✕ or Esc). map.search runs the same search from the command line or a script, and goes to a hit with "go":

rustgis-cli run --sample --cmd 'map.search={"query":"Tokyo","go":true}'

Right-click the map for a menu about that place. Its first row shows the place in the status bar's coordinate format (latitude and longitude as 40.712800, -74.006000 for decimal degrees); click it to copy it. North of 84° N and south of 80° S, where UTM and MGRS have no zone, and on the map of another body than the Earth, it shows and copies the latitude and longitude, and its hover text says why. Below it, Copy As GeoJSON copies the place as a point feature, Center Map Here and Zoom In Here move the view, and Directions To Here and Directions From Here open the Directions pane. Quick Analysis runs a tool at the place: a buffer of 500 m, 1 km or 5 km (¼, 1 or 5 miles when the Directions pane is set to miles; gp.bufferPoint), a viewshed of 2, 5 or 15 km or a line of sight on an elevation raster in the map, and Open In Geoprocessing… opens the Buffer tool. View In Google Maps and View In Google Earth open the place in your browser at about the same zoom.

Coordinate readouts¶

The status bar shows the pointer's coordinates while it is over the map, and keeps the last position (dimmed) when it leaves, so you can move to the status bar and click it. It reads — until the pointer has been over the map, and again after you switch to another map or open another project. Click the coordinates to step through four formats; the choice is remembered and also sets the right-click menu's first row. The same place in each:

Format Example Notes
Decimal degrees 15.3000° E 49.8500° N four decimal places
DMS 15°18′00.0″ E 49°51′00.0″ N degrees, minutes and seconds
UTM 33north 521567 5521996 zone and hemisphere (33south … south of the equator), easting and northing in metres, rounded to the metre
MGRS 33U WR 21566 21996 zone (two digits: 04Q FJ …) and band, the 100 km square, easting and northing within it, truncated to the metre

UTM states the hemisphere, as GeographicLib's GeoConvert -u -l writes it, not the latitude band: in plain UTM a letter after the zone is commonly read as the hemisphere, so 38S (band S, 32° N to 40° N) would be taken for the southern hemisphere, 10,000 km away. Latitude bands are MGRS's. UTM and MGRS are on WGS 84, with the zone exceptions for southern Norway (32V) and Svalbard (31X, 33X, 35X and 37X), and cover 80° S to 84° N. Nearer the poles they would need the Universal Polar Stereographic grid, which RustGIS doesn't have: there the readout shows No UTM here (or No MGRS here) rather than a wrong reference, and so it does on the map of another body than the Earth, where degrees still read. Scripts set the format with ui.coordinateFormat={"format":"utm"} (decimal, dms, utm or mgrs); without format it steps to the next one. A saved format this version doesn't know, say from a newer RustGIS, reads as decimal degrees with a notice, and stays saved until you pick a format.

The search box takes the same grid references, in any case:

  • MGRS with or without spaces: 33UWR2156621996, 33U WR 21566 21996, 33UWR 2156 2199, or a 100 km square alone, 33UWR; a zone below 10 with or without its leading zero (04QFJ, 4QFJ). The digits are an even count from 0 to 10, half for the easting and half for the northing, and the search goes to the middle of the square they name. A 100 km square is also looked for in the attribute tables, since Sentinel-2 tiles are named after them.
  • UTM as the readout writes it, zone and hemisphere: 33north 521567 5521996, 21south 371625 6170423 (also with a space, 33 north …); or zone and latitude band: 33U 521567 5521996. 33N reads as the northern hemisphere when the northing isn't in band N, since both readings agree. S after the zone is always band S (32° N to 40° N), as in 11S 384629 3768405 (Los Angeles); with a northing outside band S it could mean the southern hemisphere, so it finds nothing: write south instead.

A reference that doesn't exist (square letters the zone doesn't use, a northing or a square outside the band, an odd number of digits, a place outside the zone) finds nothing rather than a place somewhere else, and the list says why; map.search returns the reason as gridError. A 100 km square written with spaces, such as 5 Ave or 4K TV, is as likely ordinary text, so one that names no square gives no reason; written as one word, 38TMJ, it does. On the map of another body, grid references find nothing.

Directions¶

Directions (Home › Navigate) plans a route between places and follows it turn by turn, with the public Valhalla server run by FOSSGIS on OpenStreetMap data. Pick Drive, Bike or Walk, then set the start and the destination: type an address (Enter looks it up with Nominatim) or a latitude and longitude, press the ⌖ button and click the map, or right-click the map and choose Directions From Here or Directions To Here. + Add Stop adds places on the way (up to 20 in all), Swap reverses the trip, and a drive can avoid tolls, highways or ferries; Miles gives distances in miles and feet.

Routes are drawn as soon as every place is set. Between two places the server offers up to two other ways, drawn grey: pick one in the list or click it on the map. The steps list each maneuver with its arrow, instruction and distance; click one to zoom to it. Simulate replays the route as a drive the map follows, with the next maneuver and its distance in a banner across the top of the map, at 1× to 16× speed (pause, drag the slider to jump along, End to stop). Save as Layer adds the route as a line layer, and its maneuvers as a point layer, to style, export (GPX, GeoJSON…) or keep with the project.

The first time, the pane asks before anything is sent: planning a route sends its places to the routing server, and looking a place up sends its name to Nominatim. Travel times don't include live traffic, and the public server is shared, so it answers light use only.

map.route plans a route from the command line, a script or the AI Assistant, with places given as [lon, lat], "lat, lon" or a name to look up, and adds it and its steps as layers; ui.pane.directions with from, to (and via, mode) opens the pane on a route. map.route sends its places to the same public servers (names to Nominatim, at most five a call, a second apart; coordinates to FOSSGIS's Valhalla), so the AI Assistant asks before running it:

rustgis-cli run --sample --cmd 'map.route={"from":"Brandenburg Gate, Berlin","to":"Alexanderplatz, Berlin","mode":"bike"}'

On the web, plan routes in the Directions pane; map.route cannot fetch a route or look up place names there. On the desktop, its optional url selects another Valhalla server.

Basemaps¶

Home › Add › Basemap offers five built-in basemaps drawn from a world outline (Paper, Midnight, Ocean, Atlas, Blank), which need no network, and web tile layers over them:

  • Raster tiles from an XYZ or TMS template (https://…/{z}/{x}/{y}.png; {-y} for rows from the south, {s} for subdomains, {q} for Bing-style quadkeys) or a WMS through {bbox-epsg-3857}. PNG, JPEG and WebP.
  • Vector tiles (Mapbox Vector Tiles, .pbf) from a template or a TileJSON address, drawn in a built-in style that knows the OpenMapTiles, Protomaps and Shortbread schemas.
  • PMTiles archives (.pmtiles) of raster or vector tiles: a web address, read a tile at a time by byte ranges (the server must allow range requests, and CORS for the web app), or on the desktop a file (Tile Archive File…, Add Data, or drop it on the window). Version 3 archives are read, uncompressed or with gzip, Brotli or Zstandard. The map moves to what the archive covers once its header is read, unless it covers the whole world, and right-clicking its row in Layers gives Zoom To Layer. A vector archive that is a basemap is drawn in the built-in style; any other is drawn with each of its layers in a colour of its own.
  • MBTiles files (.mbtiles) of raster or vector tiles, drawn as PMTiles archives are: on the desktop read in place a tile at a time (Tile Archive File…, Add Data, or drop it on the window); in the web app added with Add Data and held in memory for the session (a project keeps the layer, and asks for the file again).
  • Map styles: a MapLibre style document (style.json), with its vector, raster and elevation (raster-dem: Terrarium, Mapbox or custom encoding) sources. RustGIS draws background, fill, line, circle and raster layers, labels with their icons from the style's sprites (SDF icons in their icon-color; the icons of a sprite in the style's list are named <sprite id>:<name>, those of a sprite with the id default just <name>), and fill patterns; it evaluates the style's expressions and zoom functions, image among them (an icon's name when a sprite has it, so coalesce falls back to the next). When the style draws images, a notice says why any are missing: a sprite at an address RustGIS won't fetch, an entry of the list that can't be read, or a sprite whose download failed, which loses only its own icons and patterns and is tried again. From elevation it draws color-relief layers (a color-relief-color that is an interpolate or step on ["elevation"]) and hillshade layers (each hillshade-method, lit as the map is drawn, north up; slopes are steepened at small scales so relief stays visible). Its shading follows the style's colours, exaggeration and light rather than matching MapLibre's pixel for pixel. Other color-relief-color expressions aren't drawn, and a notice names the layers skipped. If a style's elevation source can't be read, its relief and hillshade layers are left out with a notice saying why, and the rest is drawn. Labels are placed as MapLibre places them: at points, in the middle of areas, and along lines (street names every symbol-spacing, turned to the street, where it bends less than text-max-angle; shields and one-way arrows), wrapped to text-max-width, the style's later layers first and then by symbol-sort-key, kept apart by text-padding and the *-optional, *-allow-overlap and *-ignore-placement rules. Text along a line is drawn straight rather than curved round its bends. Line patterns and 3D extrusion are not drawn (extrusions draw flat). A label shows the lines of its text that the map's font can draw: a style's "Syria / سوريا" shows as "Syria". A style's root center, zoom, bearing and pitch say where it opens: map.addTiles with "view": true takes the map there once the style has loaded.

Present PMTiles metadata must be complete, decodable and a JSON object; malformed metadata or a stored metadata section over 16 MiB refuses ordinary loading, repair and reload rather than silently losing credits or source-layer information. RustGIS's PMTiles writer enforces the same metadata limit. This is not a limit on the archive's total size; absent metadata remains supported. MBTiles metadata names and values must be text: unreadable or NULL metadata cells also produce an error rather than being silently skipped. Native MBTiles loading, repair and reload refuse more than 1,000 metadata rows rather than silently omitting later rows.

HTTPS tiles use platform certificate trust on desktop and the command line, including private CAs trusted by the operating system. The web app uses the browser.

Tile addresses are checked after filling their placeholders, before cached bytes or network requests are used. In the native app, a document on the internet cannot select a local destination IP. Explicitly selected self-hosted servers remain supported, including ordinary hostnames resolved by local DNS, with local fallback when the same hostname also returns public addresses. Native fetchers filter the resolved IPs before connecting, using the supplying document's actual connection reach. Native requests refuse link-local and unspecified addresses, including those represented by IPv4-mapped IPv6 addresses or the well-known NAT64 prefix. Public IPv4 destinations under that prefix can pass. Document redirects also change the base for relative references. IPv4 decoding covers mapped addresses and the well-known 64:ff9b::/96 prefix. It does not inspect destinations behind 6to4 tunnels or custom translation gateways, including the RFC 8215 64:ff9b:1::/48 prefix, whose translation is network-specific. Such routes can hide a private or link-local IPv4 destination behind a public-looking IPv6 address; restrictions on those routes require network firewall policy. Native redirects are checked at each hop. In the web app, use the final service address: tile, style, TileJSON and sprite redirects, including HTTP-to-HTTPS upgrades and CDN hops, are refused because the browser hides their destinations from the app. The same browser transport refuses redirects for downloads, fonts, archive range reads and HEAD requests for file metadata; see network downloads. The web app checks the URL's spelling and normalized address, but cannot inspect resolved IPs. A public-looking hostname can reach a private or link-local server if the browser permits it; DNS resolution, local-network permissions and certificate trust are controlled by the browser. Native tile and document requests, and file downloads through From URL…, connect directly without an environment HTTP proxy, so a proxy cannot resolve destinations beyond these checks. See network downloads if your network requires a proxy.

The gallery's Web Basemaps need no API key: the OpenFreeMap vector maps (Liberty, Bright, Positron, Dark, Fiord) and USGS Imagery. Choosing one replaces the other web basemaps; tick Stack on the current basemaps above them to add it over the others instead (clicking one that already shows then takes it off). Toggling the checkbox keeps the gallery open so you can choose a basemap; choosing one closes it. For many more (OpenStreetMap, Esri, NASA and others), follow Install the Basemaps plugin under them, which opens the Plugin Manager at the Basemaps plugin; it adds a tab of public basemaps. Add any other with Add Tile Layer… at the bottom of the Basemap gallery. The gallery lists the map's tile layers: show or hide them, change their opacity and order, or remove them. The Layers pane lists them too, at their place in the drawing order: a basemap under the map's layers and above the built-in basemap; an overlay (a WMS or WMTS layer, a vector tile service, Overlay ticked in Add Tile Layer) on top of the map's layers when added, as every new layer goes. Drag one, or right-click it and choose Move up or Move down, to put it between the map's layers (above a layer group, never inside one). Tick one to show or hide it, and right-click it for Zoom To Layer (when its bounds are known), opacity, Blend mode, order and Remove; a WMS or map service layer also has Draw as: one picture of the view or tiles of a size (see One picture of the view, or tiles). A blend mode (Multiply, Screen, Overlay, Darken, Lighten, Difference) combines a tile layer with what is drawn beneath it (tile layers, the basemap, and map layers when it's placed over them): stack imagery over a hillshade and set it to Multiply to let the terrain show through. Overlays stay over the basemaps; choosing a built-in basemap removes the web basemaps but keeps the overlays. Tile layers are saved with the project, and their credits are shown in the corner of the map and drawn on exports.

Use Explore to click features in vector PMTiles, MBTiles or MVT layers added as data and see their source-layer names and attributes in a pop-up. This works with Topmost Layer or Visible Layers pop-up mode, not with catalog basemaps or MapLibre styles; with Selected in Layers, click a tile layer's row to select it and a click on the map identifies that layer alone, whichever it is (a WMS layer too). Tiles are not selectable or editable; a tile not yet loaded may need another click once it arrives. Reload in the Basemap gallery (map.reloadWebLayers) clears cached tiles and styles and fetches them again.

Reloading a local tile archive (desktop). After changing a PMTiles or MBTiles file on disk, right-click its row in Layers and choose Reload Data (Reload Tile Archive in Command Search). CLI, Python, MCP and the Assistant use map.reloadTiles={"index":0}; "name" or catalog "id" can select the archive instead. No replacement path is accepted. The desktop app also watches the archive (and an MBTiles file's -wal and -journal) and marks its row (changed on disk) with a notification offering Reload Data; see Changed on disk. RustGIS doesn't reload it until you choose. Reload rereads that archive's metadata and tiles without clearing other archives or web layers. The layer keeps its name, visibility, opacity, blend, drawing order, source-layer visibility, style and other presentation settings; the map's view stays where it is. Undo cannot restore file contents that another program has overwritten.

On the desktop, menu actions and direct attached MCP run_command calls validate reload and repair on a background worker; the status bar shows the work and Cancel. Only one read runs at a time. If its project, map, archive or presentation changes before it lands, the result is left unapplied: retry on the intended target. A paused attached batch also rejects its remaining commands if the project or selected map changes. Notebook cells, task steps, scripts (the Script pane and attached MCP run) and file-bridge scripts validate inline on the window thread, without reload status or cancellation during validation. Cancelling MBTiles validation also interrupts SQLite queries used to infer missing zoom metadata. It cannot preempt a filesystem operation blocked inside the operating system.

Approval binds the selected archive's owning map, stack position and source route, not just its name or index. Retargeting or reordering it requires reviewing the operation again.

Each operation observes file size and modification time before validation, checks them again after validation and immediately before applying the result, and rejects observable changes. It also checks file identity and change time where the platform exposes them, plus MBTiles -wal and -journal existence and revisions (not the reader-coordination -shm file). Metadata that cannot be read is an error, not permission to apply an unchecked result. These checks are not an immutable snapshot: a rewrite that leaves the same observations, or a write after the final check, may escape detection. Finish writing before reloading or repairing. If an archive is reloaded during a map or layout export, the export refuses a mixed-version result. Wait for the reload to finish and retry the export.

Reload first checks that the file can be read. If it is missing, unreadable or invalid, a notice explains the failure and the existing layer and retained cached data are left unchanged. Finish writing or restore the file, then choose Reload Data again. If the file has moved, choose Repair Data Source… instead and select its new location. Repair also checks the replacement before changing the source; cancelling or choosing an unreadable or invalid archive leaves the existing source and layer unchanged, so you can retry with a valid file.

Validation reads one size-bounded raster sample from a fresh MBTiles connection and decodes it before dropping retained caches. PMTiles validation checks all reachable index directories, not every tile payload. It allows at most 64 MiB stored and 64 MiB decoded index bytes, 1,048,576 index entries, 4,096 unique directories (including the root), and four leaf levels; each stored directory read is limited to 16 MiB. An invalid index or an exceeded validation budget is an explicit error without eviction, even if an unusually large index is otherwise valid.

Neither action is available for local archives in the web app, or for remote tile services. On Windows, an open MBTiles file may not be replaceable while RustGIS holds it open: reload does not bypass that operating-system restriction. Close RustGIS before replacing the file, then reopen the project, or write to a different path and use Repair Data Source….

Source layers. A vector PMTiles or MBTiles archive lists its source layers (the vector_layers its metadata names: water, roads, pois…) under its row in the Layers pane: the arrow beside it opens them, each with its own checkbox. Untick one to hide what it draws (fills, lines, points and labels, in the default look and in a style given with styleLayers alike); Explore's pop-ups then skip its features too. From scripts, map.tiles lists a vector archive's sourceLayers once its header is read, and map.setTiles takes "hiddenLayers": ["roads", …], the source layers switched off ([] or null shows them all again). Switching one off is an edit to undo, and a project keeps which are off.

Tiles load in the background and the map fills in as they arrive, with coarser tiles standing in meanwhile; exports and rustgis-cli wait for them. On the globe and in other projections the tiles are reprojected. From scripts: map.addTiles, map.removeTiles, map.setTiles, map.zoomToTiles and map.tiles. map.addTiles accepts optional "bounds": [west, south, east, north] in longitude/latitude; service.add retains the bounds advertised by map and tile services. Explicit bounds must form a finite, ordered geographic rectangle; an invalid value rejects the command without adding or replacing a layer. Invalid or malformed bounds in a saved project are instead discarded, leaving the project and tile layer usable without that coverage metadata.

Legends. A WMS or WMTS layer added from its service has its legend in the Layers pane, as ArcGIS Pro's Contents pane shows a map service layer's: the arrow beside it opens the legend under the layer, indented as a feature layer's classes are, at the size the service draws it (one wider than the pane scrolls across). A WMS layer's legend is the one its service draws from the layer's styling (GetLegendGraphic, when the service offers it in PNG or JPEG), else the picture of its default style (its LegendURL: the style named default, else its first, inherited from the layers above it as WMS has it); when one can't be shown the next is. A WMTS layer's comes from its default style, which may give one per range of scales: the one for the map's scale shows, and "No legend at this scale" where none is for it. The pictures are fetched when first shown and kept (one that couldn't be fetched is asked for again a minute later). Any tile layer can be given one: map.addTiles and map.setTiles take "legend" (an address, or a list of addresses and {"url", "minScale", "maxScale"}; null takes it away), and map.expandTiles shows or hides it (as layer.expand does a layer's classes: not an edit to undo). A project keeps a layer's legends and whether they show.

rustgis-cli run --cmd 'service.add={"url":"https://wms.geo.admin.ch/","layer":"ch.swisstopo.geologie-swissgeocover2d_lines"}' --cmd 'map.tiles={}'

Other worlds¶

A map can be on the Moon, Mars or another body instead of the Earth. Pick the body from the planet button at the top of the Layers pane, or under Celestial Bodies in the Basemap gallery: Earth, Moon, Mars, Callisto, Charon, Europa, Ganymede, Io, Mercury, Pluto, Titan and Venus. Choosing one closes the gallery and puts one of its basemaps under the map (reopen the gallery for the others: Viking, MOLA elevation and hillshade mosaics for Mars, LOLA albedo for the Moon, and the USGS Astrogeology mosaics of the rest) and measures on that body: the Measure tools, the scale bar, the map scale and layout scale bars use its radius. Coordinates stay longitude and latitude, and the map stays a Web Mercator sphere, the size of the body. Off the Earth there is no atmosphere: the globe has no glow around it, and a tilted view's sky is the black of space, with no haze toward the horizon. Choosing the Earth replaces all non-overlay web tile layers, including planetary and custom basemaps, with USGS Imagery, keeping overlay tile layers. The body is saved with the map.

The basemaps come from OpenPlanetaryMap and USGS Astrogeology. OpenPlanetaryMap's mosaics sit in storage that browsers can't read from another site, and USGS serves its mosaics in lon/lat only, so both are read through the opengeos tile service (tiles.geolibre.app, shared with GeoLibre), which adds what browsers need, reprojects the USGS mosaics and caches the tiles. From scripts: map.body={"name": "mars"} ("basemap" picks another of its basemaps, or false adds none, still taking other bodies' basemaps off) and map.bodies.

Worldview¶

The world outline and the World Atlas sample's countries show Natural Earth's default (de facto) borders, which are built in, or the borders as one of 31 countries sees them: Natural Earth's points of view, from Argentina to Vietnam. Choose one in Settings › Language › Worldview, set RUSTGIS_WORLDVIEW to its code (such as chn, ind or usa), or pass --worldview to rustgis-cli.

A point of view isn't part of the app: it is downloaded from source.coop when you choose it, and the desktop app and the command line keep it in the cache folder, so it is downloaded once; the web app downloads it again in each session. Detail, at the top of the same menu, sets how much:

  • Simplified (the default): borders at 1:110m, like the built-in ones; about 250 KB.
  • Original: Natural Earth's 1:10m borders with every island and territory; a 3 MB download, 10 MB once unpacked, so maps with it draw more slowly. On a flat Web Mercator map the basemap's land outline is drawn simplified to within a pixel at each zoom, which keeps most of that cost away from it; the globe and other projections draw it in full. With the default worldview, this downloads the detailed version of the built-in borders.

rustgis-cli takes --worldview-detail original. The map keeps the borders it shows until the download arrives, and says so if it fails. Until you choose one, RustGIS shows China's view when the system's time zone is mainland China's (such as Asia/Shanghai) or its first locale is zh-CN, downloading it the same way, and the default borders everywhere else. Your choice is kept for the next start. An untouched sample project switches its countries when the new borders arrive; others keep the layer they have.

Projections¶

The flat map is drawn in Web Mercator by default. View › Projection switches it to another projection, and clicking the coordinate system in the status bar opens the Coordinate System dialog, which also takes a projected EPSG code (such as 3035, 32633 for UTM zone 33N or 2056 for Switzerland) or an ESRI code, the one .prj files and ArcGIS name (such as ESRI:102033 for South America Albers Equal Area Conic, or ESRI:54030 for World Robinson). A code whose projection method or datum grid RustGIS can't use (Laborde such as 8441, or a NAD27 code that needs a grid) is refused with the reason, rather than drawn in another projection. RustGIS supports a subset of Esri's projected systems, checked against PROJ at representative points; unsupported codes such as World Miller (ESRI:54003) and the two-point oblique Mercators (ESRI:53025, ESRI:54025) are refused as unknown codes. The presets:

Preset Kind
Equirectangular, Miller World, cylindrical
Mollweide, Lambert Cylindrical Equal Area, Equal Earth, Eckert IV World, equal-area
Robinson, Natural Earth, Winkel Tripel World, compromise
North / South Polar Stereographic Polar
Europe Equal Area (ETRS89 LAEA), USA Contiguous Albers Equal Area Regional, equal-area
Albers Equal Area Conic, Lambert Conformal Conic Conic, for a mid-latitude region
VN-2000 / TM-3 (Vietnam), VN-2000 / UTM zones 48N and 49N Vietnam, transverse Mercator

The world and polar presets take a central meridian, and the two conics also their standard parallels: pick the preset in the Coordinate System dialog and set Central meridian (and First and Second standard parallel) beside it. A Pacific-centred Robinson is map.projection {"type": "robinson", "lon_0": 150}, and an Albers for South America {"type": "albers", "lon_0": -55, "lat_1": -5, "lat_2": -35}. Without type, the values go to the map's current preset. The map saves them in its code (robinson +lon_0=150), which can also be typed as it is. View › Projection picks a preset in its standard form, and marks the one in use whatever its parameters. Meridians run from -180° to 180° and parallels from -89° to 89°. Parallels that average within 0.5° of the equator (mirrored, such as -30° and 30°, or both next to it) make no cone and are refused, as is a value a preset doesn't take. The two regional grids keep their standard definitions. A conic's area of use runs 35° beyond its parallels and 90° either side of its meridian.

For Vietnam, VN-2000 / TM-3 takes a central meridian (105°E by default), with province shortcut buttons in the Coordinate System dialog. A province alias such as vn2000:hanoi sets its meridian; scripts can use map.projection={"type":"vn2000tm3","lon_0":105.75}. The UTM presets use fixed zones: vn2000Utm48 (EPSG:3405) and vn2000Utm49 (EPSG:3406). These systems include the seven-parameter datum shift to WGS 84. A bare VN-2000 is ambiguous and is refused: name TM-3 or a UTM zone.

Layers are projected on the fly. A feature that crosses the edge of a world projection (the meridian opposite its central one, such as 90°E in Equal Earth Americas) is cut there and drawn on both sides of the map. Each projection has an area of use: polar projections show their hemisphere poleward of 30°, regional ones their region, transverse Mercator (UTM) zones 30° either side of their meridian, and data outside it isn't drawn. Switching keeps the centre and scale, and the choice is saved with the map (map.projection from the command line). A raster already in the map's coordinate system draws without being reprojected. To open every new session in a projection, set it in Settings › Start-up › Projection. Robinson, Equal Earth, Natural Earth, Winkel Tripel and Eckert IV are computed by RustGIS itself on a sphere, so EPSG:8857 (Equal Earth) works too. So is the sinusoidal projection, on a sphere or an ellipsoid: MODIS rasters, whose GeoTIFFs give it as their own projection, open and draw, and World, Sphere and Africa Sinusoidal (ESRI:54008, 53008, 102011) can be the map's.

Globe¶

View › Globe shows the map as a 3D globe seen from space instead of the flat Web Mercator map, and back again. Drag to spin the globe, right-drag or Ctrl+drag to turn and tilt it (as on the flat map), scroll to zoom, and use Full Extent to see the whole Earth. Every layer draws on the globe: vectors are clipped at the horizon and follow great circles, rasters are draped onto the sphere, and labels, selection, identify, measuring and editing work as on the flat map. The scale stays the same when you switch, and the choice is saved with each map in the project (map.projection from the command line).

While the globe turns or zooms, the map is drawn flat once and laid over the sphere by the graphics card, so it moves smoothly whatever the basemap; labels come back, and the map is drawn exactly, as soon as it stops.

The globe hangs in dark space, in the light theme too, with stars around it and now and then a shooting star passing behind it. View › Visual Effect › Stars turns them off. Exported maps and layout map frames print the globe in a light space instead, without stars, so a globe inset suits a light page (on the Midnight basemap the space stays dark).

View effects¶

The View tab adds effects drawn over the map: Illumination (day and night shading for a time, with twilight), Weather (haze, fog, rain, snow), Visual Effect (vignette, edges, the stars around the globe), Camera Effect (tilt-shift blur, film grain) and Postprocessing (brightness, contrast, saturation, sepia). The Color Vision simulator shows the map as people with protanopia, deuteranopia, tritanopia or achromatopsia see it (on screen only; exports are untouched). View › Display › Graticule (map.graticule) draws a graticule over the basemap; its arrow picks the type, and picking one shows it:

  • Latitude and Longitude (the default): parallels and meridians, more of them as you zoom in.
  • Measured Grid: eastings and northings in a projected coordinate system, at a round interval that suits the scale (about eight lines across the view), as a layout's measured grid draws them. The system is the one typed under Measured grid's coordinate system (an EPSG code such as 2056, WKT or a PROJ string; "crs" in the command); left empty, it is the map's own when the map is projected, else the WGS 84 UTM zone at the centre of the view. A system that can't be read or is geographic is refused, with a message naming it.
  • UTM Zones: the WGS 84 UTM zones and their latitude bands (C to X, 80° S to 84° N), with southern Norway's and Svalbard's wider zones. Once no more than eight zone-and-band areas are in view, each also gets its own eastings and northings, at the same interval in all of them and cut at the zone's edges.

The grids have no labels on the map; the status bar reads coordinates in UTM or MGRS. Like the latitudes and longitudes, they aren't drawn on the copies of the world a flat map shows past ±180°. The type and system are saved with the map; a project whose measured grid names a system this version can't use (one in degrees, or a PROJ string the web app can't read) opens without it, and says so.

Global shows a globe; Local a flat view centred where you are (azimuthal equidistant; not in the web app, which doesn't include that projection). Wireframe draws polygons as outlines; Raise Priority and Lower Priority change a layer's drawing priority independently of its order. View Clipping hides features beyond a distance from the centre, and Speed Display draws without antialiasing. The Navigator adds a compass, zoom buttons and a pan pad; Camera shows and sets the position and scale; the Magnifier shows twice the map under the pointer. On the Home tab, First-Person navigation walks with W A S D and flies up and down with Q and E. The interface follows the system's light or dark setting; View › Display › Dark Mode switches it by hand.

View › Display › GPU Rendering (beta) draws supported parts of the flat map on the GPU for smoother navigation on large datasets. Unsupported geometry and layer effects fall back to the CPU. Raster tiles and opaque vector tile styles can be drawn directly on the GPU on a north-up Web Mercator map; translucent or blended tiles use cached pictures during navigation.

GPU Rendering is off by default and remembered across sessions (ui.map.gpu is the command; RUSTGIS_GPU=1 forces it on for development). The globe, view effects and colour-vision simulation stay on the CPU, and exports are unaffected. A rotated or tilted map uses the GPU only when every visible layer can be drawn there: tiles, rasters, terrain, transparency, masking, swipe, blending, aggregation, graphics, stacked strokes, fills or labels with icons keep it on the CPU. Extrusions use the GPU only on a tilted map. In the desktop app, if the graphics card runs out of memory (a card with 2 GB at a large window size, with other programs using it too), the map switches to the CPU, with a notice, instead of the app closing; clicking CPU in the status bar tries the GPU again, as does the next start (the setting stays on). Any other error the graphics card reports is said once, as the map (or, with the map on the CPU, the window) may then be drawn wrongly.
